# Behavioral economics of saving and goal commitment (Africa and emerging markets), for MoloPay UX

Scope: experimentally measured effects that can justify UX features for a goal-based savings-to-purchase app (MoloPay, Cameroon: save toward a product at a frozen price, then receive it; plus planned payments like rent). Users are low/modest-income mobile money users in Douala and Yaoundé who know tontines/njangi.

Note on sourcing: about 20 searches/fetches were run. Several figures come from J-PAL/IPA summaries or paper abstracts, not the full papers; this is flagged where relevant. Widely cited numbers that could not be checked against a source this session are listed under Gaps, not under Cited Findings.

---

## 1. Commitment devices: take-up, effects, demand and downsides

### Takeaway
Commitment savings products are taken up by a minority (about 20-30% of those offered) but have large effects on those offered: +81-82% balances in the Philippines (SEED), +66% to +128% health savings in Kenya, and +17% household spending in Malawi. Hard commitment can backfire: in the Philippines 55% of clients defaulted on a self-chosen installment contract, and in Uganda students saved more under soft commitment (cash payout) than hard (voucher). For MoloPay, a "goal lock" works best as a labeled, mildly restricted pot with a pressure-relief exit, not a harsh penalty.

### Cited Findings
**SEED, Philippines (Ashraf, Karlan & Yin 2006, QJE 121(2): 635-72)**
- Sample: 1,777 clients of Green Bank of Caraga (Mindanao). SEED restricted withdrawals until a self-chosen date or amount was reached, and paid the same 4% interest as a regular account, so restriction was the only added feature — [J-PAL summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/print/pdf/node/1423?lang=en); [Working paper](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/nanna/record/28411/files/dp050917.pdf)
- Take-up: 28% of those explicitly offered SEED opened an account — [J-PAL summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/print/pdf/node/1423?lang=en)
- Effect: after 12 months, average savings balances rose 81 percentage points in the treatment group vs the comparison group (working paper). J-PAL reports +82% after one year and +42% after six months. The gap is likely due to working-paper vs. published or summary versions — [Working paper](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/nanna/record/28411/files/dp050917.pdf); [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/print/pdf/node/1423?lang=en)

**Safe box / Lock box / Health Pot, western Kenya (Dupas & Robinson 2013, AER, "Why Don't the Poor Save More?")**
- Sample: 113 ROSCAs and 771 individuals randomized into four products or a comparison group — [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/constraints-saving-health-expenditures-kenya)
- Safe Box (a simple metal box; the participant holds the key and can withdraw any time): preventive health spending +66%, from about US$3.43 (KES 257) to US$5.70 per year — [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/constraints-saving-health-expenditures-kenya)
- Lock Box (key held by program officer, so a hard commitment): no effect on preventive health spending overall. It was used more by the 16% of the sample who give financial help to friends and relatives, which fits "shield money from others" — [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/constraints-saving-health-expenditures-kenya)
- Health Pot (a ROSCA-style pot earmarked for health, adding credit and social pressure): preventive health spending +128%, to US$7.85. Present-biased individuals did benefit here, unlike in the box arms — [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/constraints-saving-health-expenditures-kenya)
- Health Savings Account (money earmarked for emergencies): recipients were more likely to afford treatment when ill — [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/constraints-saving-health-expenditures-kenya)
- Persistence after 3 years: 39% still using the box (Safe/Lock); 48% (Health Pot) and 53% (HSA) still participating — [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/constraints-saving-health-expenditures-kenya)
- Present bias: about 16% of the sample were present-biased, and they did not benefit from the Safe Box or Lock Box — [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/constraints-saving-health-expenditures-kenya)
- Caveat: the NBER abstract says earmarking for preventive health reduced savings on average because the liquidity cost was too high. This conflicts in emphasis with the J-PAL Health Pot result and should be checked in the paper before citing — [search summary of NBER w17255](https://causalclaims.trfetzer.com/paper/w17255.html)

**Commitment accounts, rural Malawi (Brune, Giné, Goldberg & Yang; NBER WP 20946, 2015; published EDCC 2016)**
- Sample: about 3,150-3,900 tobacco farmers in clubs of 10-15, randomized to ordinary accounts, ordinary plus commitment accounts, or comparison — [NBER WP](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20946/w20946.pdf); [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/fr/print/pdf/node/1585)
- Only the commitment treatment had significant effects: +0.42 acres cultivated (vs a 4.3-acre mean), +26% input use, +17% household expenditure in the last 30 days. One source reports +22% crop output value (fragmentary snippet). Ordinary accounts alone had no significant effects — [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/fr/print/pdf/node/1585); [NBER WP](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20946/w20946.pdf)
- Mechanism: commitment-group farmers kept most of their money in ordinary accounts, not in the locked ones. The authors attribute the gains to keeping funds from being shared with one's social network, not to the lock itself — [NBER WP](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20946/w20946.pdf)

**Downsides and failures of hard commitment**
- Philippines, "When Commitment Fails" (John, Management Science 2020, 66(2): 503-529): clients chose a regular installment savings contract with a self-chosen default penalty. The intent-to-treat effect on bank savings was large, but 55% of clients defaulted on their contract. The best-supported explanation: people chose penalties too small to overcome their self-control problems (partial sophistication). Take-up and default both correlate negatively with measures of sophisticated hyperbolic discounting — [IPA PDF](https://poverty-action.org/sites/default/files/publications/John_When_Commitment_Fails.pdf); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cep/stieop/055.html)
- Uganda, "Loose Knots" (Karlan & Linden; JDE 2025): school savings in 136 schools in eastern Uganda. Students deposited significantly more when savings were returned as cash (soft commitment) than as vouchers for school supplies (hard commitment). Combined with parent outreach, the cash arm had higher supply ownership and +0.11 SD test scores; without parent outreach no arm had significant effects — [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/smoothing-cost-education-micro-savings-ugandan-primary-schools)
- Kenya: present-biased people did not benefit from the pure lock box (above) — [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/constraints-saving-health-expenditures-kenya)

### Inferences
- Take-up of explicit commitment is low (about 28% in SEED). MoloPay should not rely on users choosing strict locks; the product itself (pay toward a named item at a frozen price) is already a soft commitment, which is the form with the best evidence (Kenya Safe Box, Uganda soft commitment).
- Self-chosen penalties are risky: users under-set them and then default (55%). Prefer non-monetary friction (cooling-off delay, a "talk to your goal" confirmation screen, or withdrawal that cancels the price freeze) over cash penalties. Losing the frozen price is a natural, easy-to-understand stake.
- The Malawi and Kenya results suggest that much of the value of a lock in Africa is shielding money from family and network requests. A UX line such as "this money is reserved for your [product], it can't be lent out" can serve as a socially acceptable excuse to refuse.
- ROSCA-like social structure (Health Pot) helped present-biased people where solo boxes did not. That supports group or "njangi-style" goal features.

### Gaps
- Exact SEED effects on later horizons (2.5-year follow-up) and the fraction of SEED accounts reaching goal were not retrieved.
- Per-arm sample sizes for Dupas & Robinson were not retrieved, and the conflict between J-PAL and the NBER abstract on earmarking was not resolved.
- Take-up rate of the commitment account in Malawi (often cited around 20%) was not verified.

## 2. Labeled savings, mental accounting and earmarking

### Takeaway
Labeling and partitioning money toward a specific goal raises savings among low-income people. In India, splitting savings into two earmarked envelopes roughly doubled savings in the high-target group. In Kenya, a tangible tracking object (a gold coin) more than doubled savings vs SMS-only. In Peru, reminders that named the user's labeled goal raised savings by about 16%, while generic reminders did nothing. Effects of digital locks in Kenya (M-Shwari) are real during promotions but did not persist afterward.

### Cited Findings
- **Soman & Cheema (2011, Journal of Marketing Research), "Earmarking and Partitioning"**: field experiment with low-income construction laborers in rural India paid weekly in cash. For 15 weeks, social workers put earmarked savings in envelopes each Saturday. Arms varied savings target (low or high), one sealed envelope vs two, and plain envelopes vs envelopes with photos of the workers' children. Baseline savings rate averaged 0.75% in the prior six months — [Russell Sage PDF](https://www.russellsage.org/sites/default/files/u137/jmr-C-s014-s021-online.pdf); [Rotman / Soman](https://rotman.utoronto.ca/Connect/Rotman-MAG/IdeaExchange/Dilip-Soman)
- Partitioning: two envelopes saved more than one. Totals were 373 vs 269 rupees (low target) and 456 vs 211 rupees (high target). The single-envelope, high-target group saved least — [WEF summary](https://world-economic-forum.relayto.com/e/how-can-we-save-for-our-future-gllt8qadtun3a/FtbElYFC16); [Rotman](https://rotman.utoronto.ca/Connect/Rotman-MAG/IdeaExchange/Dilip-Soman)
- **Akbas, Ariely, Robalino & Weber (IZA DP 10024, 2016), Kenya**: six-month experiment with low and irregular income informal workers in a voluntary pension program, 12 conditions. Control got weekly SMS reminders and balance reports. Treatments added reminders seemingly from the participant's child, a gold coin for tracking weekly deposits, and 10% or 20% matches. The coin group saved more than twice the control group, the best result of any arm (including the cash matches). The authors attribute this to the coin acting as a tangible tracking object — [IZA](https://iza.org/publications/dp/10024); [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:iza:izadps:dp10024)
- **Karlan et al., Peru**: the only site where clients labeled their account with a specific future expense. Reminders naming that expense raised savings about 16% vs no reminder; generic reminders had no effect there — [causalclaims summary of NBER w16205](https://causalclaims.trfetzer.com/paper/w16205.html); [NBER exec summary](https://img.nber.org/conferences/2010/SI2010/SAV/Executive%20Summary%20--%20Getting%20to%20the%20Top%20of%20Mind%20-.pdf)
- **Fewer goals may help**: J-PAL hosts "Saving for multiple financial needs: evidence from Malawi"; a World Bank Development Impact post on it is titled "Marketing saving: fewer goals can lead to higher savings" — [J-PAL paper](https://povertyactionlab.org/media/file-research-paper/saving-multiple-financial-needs-evidence-malawi); [World Bank blog](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/impactevaluations/marketing-saving-fewer-goals-can-lead-to-higher-savings) (details not retrieved; see Gaps)
- **M-Shwari Lock Savings, Kenya**: FSD Kenya describes Lock Savings as a product for saving toward a longer-term goal, paying an extra 1% interest for term funds — [FSD Kenya case study](https://www.fsdkenya.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/aws/Archive%20data%20FSD/M-Shwari_Briefing-final_digital.pdf-9.pdf?_t=1611135649)
- **"High Hopes" (Habyarimana & Jack), Kenya**: parents of primary-school leavers were encouraged to open mobile-money (M-Shwari) accounts, with a lock savings option promoted to half the treatment group and SMS reminders to save for secondary education cross-randomized. Treatment raised account take-up by 25 percentage points and total financial savings rose several-fold — [IPA WP](https://poverty-action.org/sites/default/files/publications/WP004_Habyarimana.Jack_v3%20%281%29.pdf); [VoxDev](https://voxdev.org/voxdevlit/mobile-money-issue-2/impacts-mobile-money)
- **Null/transient results**: the "Stawisha na M-Shwari" promotion (2016) raised savings during the promotion but with no sustained increase afterward. A regression discontinuity study found M-Shwari loan access did not change household savings or productive assets — [VoxDev](https://voxdev.org/voxdevlit/mobile-money-issue-2/impacts-mobile-money)
- **Mozambique**: a remunerated mobile savings account raised mobile savings only while interest was being paid (and increased fertilizer investment); mobile money availability alone did not significantly raise overall savings — [Batista & Vicente, World Development](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305750X20300310); [ATAI PDF](https://www.atai-research.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/Batista-and-Vincente-mozambique-mobile-money-Dec-2018.pdf)

### Inferences
- MoloPay's core concept (one pot = one named product, with a photo) matches the earmarking evidence. Show a product photo, and optionally a personal photo ("for my daughter's school bag"), on the goal card. The child-photo arm's specific effect was not retrieved, so present this as a reasonable hypothesis, not a measured effect.
- Separate pots per goal (rent vs phone) reflect partitioning. But the Malawi "fewer goals" finding suggests limiting the number of simultaneous goals or highlighting one main goal.
- The Kenya coin result supports a strong visual progress tracker (a physical-feeling token, stamp or filling object), which may outperform small cash bonuses.
- Promotions and interest bonuses produce short-lived bumps (M-Shwari, Mozambique). Don't build retention on promotional incentives alone.

### Gaps
- Exact effect of the children's-photo envelopes in Soman & Cheema was not found in sources retrieved; the WEF partition figures are secondary.
- No digital-label RCT specifically from Cameroon or Francophone Central Africa was found.
- Full results of the Malawi multiple-goals paper were not retrieved.
- Exact magnitude of "several-fold" savings in High Hopes was not retrieved.

## 3. Reminders and SMS

### Takeaway
Reminders reliably raise savings in emerging markets, modestly on average (+6%) but much more when they name the user's specific goal (+16% in Peru) or include accountability feedback (Chile: about 3x deposits). Generic reminders can do nothing.

### Cited Findings
- **Karlan, McConnell, Mullainathan & Zinman, "Getting to the Top of Mind: How Reminders Increase Saving"** (Management Science 2016, 62(12): 3393-3411). Three field experiments in Bolivia, Peru and the Philippines with commitment-savings clients. Monthly reminders raised savings about 6% vs none; reminders were associated with higher goal attainment — [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:nbr:nberwo:16205); [causalclaims summary](https://causalclaims.trfetzer.com/paper/w16205.html); [NBER exec summary](https://img.nber.org/conferences/2010/SI2010/SAV/Executive%20Summary%20--%20Getting%20to%20the%20Top%20of%20Mind%20-.pdf)
- Goal-specific reminders: +16% in Peru; generic reminders no effect there — [causalclaims summary](https://causalclaims.trfetzer.com/paper/w16205.html)
- Reported larger effect (about 47% higher balances) for time-inconsistent savers in one analysis — [causalclaims summary](https://causalclaims.trfetzer.com/paper/w16205.html) (secondary summary, not verified against full text)
- **Kast, Meier & Pomeranz, Chile**: weekly SMS feedback on whether the participant met the deposit goal produced almost 3x as many deposits and about 7,400 pesos more saved (about 8% of monthly per capita income) over three months. Effects were similar in size to peer-group meetings — [NBER w18417 summary](https://causalclaims.trfetzer.com/paper/w18417.html); [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/use-self-help-groups-savings-commitment-device-chile)
- **Kenya (Akbas et al.)**: weekly SMS reminders plus balance reports served as the control; adding a reminder that appeared to come from the participant's child was one of the treatments. The coin, not the child-voice SMS, was the top performer — [IZA](https://iza.org/publications/dp/10024)
- **Kenya, High Hopes**: SMS reminders to save for education were cross-randomized with locked vs regular accounts — [VoxDev](https://voxdev.org/voxdevlit/mobile-money-issue-2/impacts-mobile-money) (separate reminder effect not retrieved)

### Inferences
- Reminders should name the product and show progress ("Your Tecno Spark: 62% done, 18,000 FCFA left"), not "Don't forget to save."
- Feedback ("you met / missed this week's target") seems to be the active ingredient, more than the reminder itself (Chile).
- Monthly cadence is the tested baseline; weekly feedback worked in Chile. Align with income timing (e.g., market days, salary day).

### Gaps
- No Cameroon-specific SMS savings RCT was found. Uganda SMS savings results were not retrieved.
- The separate effect of the child-voice SMS in Kenya was not retrieved.

## 4. Goal psychology: goal-gradient, endowed progress, fresh starts, defaults, social proof, prize-linked savings

### Takeaway
Foundational findings (mostly outside Africa) support progress bars, head-start credit, starting goals on salient dates, and defaults. The default effect has been replicated in an emerging market with mobile savings (Afghanistan: +40 percentage points). Peer accountability (Chile) raised deposits 3.5-fold. Prize-linked savings in South Africa raised savings by about 1% of annual income (about +40%) without cannibalizing ordinary accounts, but was ruled an illegal lottery in 2008, a legal risk to check in Cameroon.

### Cited Findings
**Goal-gradient (Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng 2006, JMR, "The Goal-Gradient Hypothesis Resurrected")**
- Café loyalty card members shortened inter-purchase time as they approached the reward; one secondary summary gives about a 20% reduction from first to last stamp. Sample sizes reported by secondary sources conflict (948 vs about 4,200 members); check the paper — [Yu-kai Chou summary](https://yukaichou.com/behavioral-analysis/goal-gradient-hypothesis-hull-kivetz-motivation-acceleration/); [Wikipedia: Goal pursuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goal_pursuit)
- Secondary source: members who did not complete cards showed no noticeable acceleration — [Yu-kai Chou summary](https://yukaichou.com/behavioral-analysis/goal-gradient-hypothesis-hull-kivetz-motivation-acceleration/)

**Endowed progress (Nunes & Drèze 2006, Journal of Consumer Research 32(4): 504-512)**
- Car wash: an 8-stamp card with 0 filled vs a 10-stamp card with 2 pre-filled (same 8 washes needed). Completion about 34% for the endowed group vs about 19% for control (as reported by secondary sources) — [UX summary](https://medium.com/usabilitygeek/design-perfect-ux-tasks-the-endowed-progress-effect-7461ca20076c); [Renascence](https://www.renascence.io/ca/behavioral-biases/endowed-progress-effect)

**Fresh start effect (Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014, Management Science 60(10): 2563-2582)**
- Three archival field studies: goal-related Google searches (e.g., "diet"), university gym visits (nearly 12,000 students' card swipes) and goal-contract sign-ups rose at temporal landmarks: start of week, month, semester, after birthdays and holidays. One summary reports about 7.5% higher gym attendance in the month after a birthday — [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:60:y:2014:i:10:p:2563-2582); [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/minds-business/why-monday-is-the-best-day-for-setting-new-goals.html); [Wharton Knowledge](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/?p=13852)

**Defaults**
- Madrian & Shea (2001, QJE 116(4): 1149-1187): 401(k) participation was significantly higher under automatic enrollment, and many new hires kept the default contribution rate and fund — [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:116:y:2001:i:4:p:1149-1187.). Secondary summaries differ on the exact numbers: one says opt-in participation was about 20% at 3 months rising to 65% at 36 months vs about 90% immediately under auto-enrollment; Duflo & Saez describe roughly 50% to over 80% — [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/savings-america-helping-individuals-provide-their-own-retirement)
- **Emerging-market replication, Afghanistan** (Blumenstock, Callen & Ghani, AER 2018, 108(10): 2868-2901, "Why Do Defaults Affect Behavior?"): 949 employees (967 in an earlier draft), mobile savings product M-Pasandaz with Afghanistan's largest mobile operator, random 0% vs 5% default salary deduction. Default enrollment raised participation by 40 percentage points. Mechanisms: present bias and cognitive cost of computing alternatives. Effects on attitudes and active saving persisted after the trial — [NBER w23590](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23590.pdf); [IPA PDF](https://poverty-action.org/sites/default/files/publications/Ghani_Defaults.pdf)
- Malawi: "Savings defaults and payment delays for cash transfers" (JDE 2017, vol. 129) also tested savings defaults in Africa — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/deveco/v129y2017icp1-13.html) (results not retrieved)

**Social norms, peer groups and accountability**
- Kast, Meier & Pomeranz, Chile ("Under-Savers Anonymous", NBER w18417): 2,687 microentrepreneurs, many Fondo Esperanza clients. Self-help peer groups grew the number of deposits 3.5-fold and almost doubled average balances. A much higher interest rate had no effect on most participants. SMS feedback replicated peer-group effects without meetings, suggesting accountability and feedback, not peers per se, drive the effect — [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/use-self-help-groups-savings-commitment-device-chile); [Russell Sage](https://www.russellsage.org/research/outputs/under-savers-anonymous-evidence-self-help-groups-and-peer-pressure-savings)

**Prize-linked savings (PLS)**
- South Africa, FNB "Million-a-Month Account" (MaMa), launched 2005; little or no interest, entry into monthly draws with 114 prizes from R1,000 to R1 million — [Kellogg Insight](https://insight.kellogg.northwestern.edu/article/using-the-lure-of-a-lottery-to-spur-savings); [FinCap summary](https://www.fincap.org.uk/en/insights/can-gambling-increase-savings)
- Cole, Iverson & Tufano ("Can Gambling Increase Savings?", Management Science 2022): holders saved about 1% of annual income more, a roughly 40% relative increase; no evidence they drained traditional accounts; PLS accounts outnumbered traditional savings accounts within 18 months; deposits slowed when national lottery jackpots rolled over (substitution with gambling); branch deposit growth rose nearly 90% the month after a local winner; traditional balances rose 4% the month after a local winner was announced; 77% kept money in traditional accounts after the program ended. Note that some participant data came from bank employees — [Kellogg Insight](https://insight.kellogg.northwestern.edu/article/using-the-lure-of-a-lottery-to-spur-savings); [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2021.4047)
- Legal: in 2008 South Africa's Supreme Court of Appeal ruled the MaMa competition an illegal lottery, which ended it — [Mail & Guardian](https://mg.co.za/article/2008-03-28-fnb-competition-an-unlawful-lottery-court-rules)
- Incentives in Africa: in Niger, a two-sided incentive worth about US$4.50 in total raised mobile money account ownership more than 200% — [search summary, Behavia](https://behavia.de/nudging-mobile-money-adoption/) (secondary)

### Inferences
- Progress bar per product goal; consider a small endowed start (e.g., first installment counted plus a welcome credit, or a 10-step track with 2 steps pre-checked when the user completes onboarding). The goal-gradient implies extra encouragement in the last 20-30% of a goal and a fast "next goal" offer after delivery (post-reward reset).
- Fresh-start prompts: suggest starting or restarting goals on the 1st of the month, on salary day, at rentrée scolaire, Ramadan/Christmas, or a birthday. Use this also for re-engaging users who missed payments ("New month, new start").
- Defaults: pre-fill a recurring automatic mobile-money debit (with clear opt-out) rather than a blank amount; the Afghanistan result shows defaults work in mobile savings in a low-income setting.
- Social accountability: optional "goal buddy" or group goals, or simply feedback messages, can replicate peer effects without social exposure.
- Prize-linked features (draws among on-time savers) have evidence but carry lottery-law risk; MoloPay would need legal review under Cameroonian gaming law before using draws.

### Gaps
- Exact Madrian & Shea figures (often cited as 37% to 86% for new hires) and Dai et al. week/month percentages were not verified against the original papers this session.
- Original-paper sample and effect sizes for Kivetz et al. not retrieved (secondary sources conflict).
- No direct evidence found on loss-aversion framing in savings in Africa, or on implementation intentions in savings in emerging markets (searches not run due to budget).
- No PLS evaluation found in Francophone Africa.

## 5. ROSCAs/tontines (njangi) and lessons for digital design

### Takeaway
ROSCAs exist largely as commitment devices: "You can't save alone" (Gugerty, Kenya, 70 ROSCAs). Building a savings product inside existing ROSCA social structures beat solo boxes for present-biased people in Kenya (Health Pot +128%). In Cameroon, tontines are historically very widespread (about half the population in the mid-1980s), but no recent national participation rate was found.

### Cited Findings
- Gugerty (2007, Economic Development and Cultural Change 55(2): 251-282): data from 70 ROSCAs in western Kenya; some ROSCAs form to provide a collective commitment mechanism for people with time-inconsistent preferences; the commitment explanation is consistent with ROSCA design and participation patterns. The title quotes a participant: "You can't save alone" — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/ecdecc/y2007v55i2p251-82.html); [IHSN catalog](https://catalog.ihsn.org/citations/59998); [Microfinance Gateway](https://microfinancegateway.org/paper/2005/01/you-cant-save-alone-commitment-rotating-savings-and-credit-associations-kenya)
- Kenya Health Pot (built inside ROSCAs) +128% preventive health spending, worked for present-biased individuals, and 48% still participated after 3 years — [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/constraints-saving-health-expenditures-kenya)
- Malawi: commitment gains came from keeping money away from the social network — [NBER WP 20946](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20946/w20946.pdf)
- Cameroon: in 1985-86, nearly half of Cameroon's population participated in at least one tontine (cited from Sandretto & Tiani 1993, in a French-language study of Anglophone Cameroon) — [core.ac.uk PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/39849538.pdf)
- Cameroon njangi/tontine groups lend only to members; the 2014 ECAM IV household survey has been used to model credit from tontines — [AOSIS / Forje study](https://library.aosis.co.za/index.php/record/view/28134); [MPRA 113629](https://ideas.repec.org/p/pra/mprapa/113629.html)
- AFP (2024): many people in Cameroon, shunned by banks, turn to tontines, which are moving to digital tools — [TechXplore / AFP](https://techxplore.com/news/2024-03-ancient-community-banking-digital-age.amp)
- Insurers target tontines in Cameroon (Sanlam offer to savings and credit associations) — [Financial Afrik](https://www.financialafrik.com/2023/01/22/cameroun-loffre-de-sanlam-assurance-aux-associations-depargne-et-de-credit/)

### Inferences
- Borrow tontine features in the UI: fixed contribution amount and rhythm ("cotisation"), a calendar of "turns", visible group progress, and a clear payout event (the product delivery as "your turn to bouffer").
- Use tontine vocabulary familiar in Douala/Yaoundé (cotisation, njangi, "bouffer") to lower cognitive load and signal commitment.
- A "group goal" or "njangi-style" mode (friends saving toward the same item or each other's items, with visible on-time status) draws on the strongest African evidence (Health Pot, Chile peer groups), while solo mode should rely on feedback messages, which replicate peer effects.
- Since commitment value partly comes from shielding money from kin, the frozen-price product pot gives users a socially acceptable reason to refuse requests.

### Gaps
- No recent nationally representative tontine participation rate for Cameroon was found (ECAM IV or later INS surveys are the likely source).
- Anderson & Baland (2002, QJE, Kenya: women use ROSCAs to protect savings from husbands) was not retrieved this session.

## 6. Practitioner organizations (ideas42, Busara, IPA, J-PAL, CGAP, BFA Global, Mastercard Foundation)

### Takeaway
