# Merchant / Partner Psychology & Economics for MoloPay (Cameroon): adoption drivers, informal credit, layaway/PAYG, BNPL, landlords & schools

Scope note: research was done in Nov 2026 with about 20 tool calls. Wherever possible, independent evidence (peer-reviewed papers, CGAP, central bank data) is kept apart from company or vendor claims, and each finding is labeled. Several requested items could not be sourced and are listed under Gaps rather than estimated.

## 1. Merchant adoption of digital payments in Africa: evidence, barriers, drivers

### Takeaway
Rigorous evidence shows strong latent merchant demand once the hassle of signing up is removed. In Kenya, 62% of restaurants wanted Lipa na M-Pesa when it was set up free and on their behalf. Network effects with customers drive adoption (Mexico, AER 2024). The main barriers are fees on thin margins, fear that revenue becomes visible to the tax authority, liquidity and settlement delays, KYC paperwork, and "not seeing the benefit." In Kenya, merchants have voted with their feet for zero-merchant-fee products (Pochi la Biashara overtook Buy Goods tills).

### Cited Findings
**Independent / academic**
- Higgins (2024, AER 114(11):3469–3512). Mexico distributed about 1 million debit cards to poor households (2009–2012) in 259 municipalities. Small retailers responded by adopting POS terminals, which led to a **21% increase in other consumers' card adoption**. Richer consumers shifted **13% of their supermarket consumption to small retailers, whose sales and profits increased**. Adoption was a coordination problem across both sides of the market. — [AER](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20201952); [Kellogg summary](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2024/financial-technology-adoption-network-externalities-of-cashless-payments-in/)
- Dalton, Pamuk, van Soest, Ramrattan & Uras (2018, DFID/Tilburg working paper), Kenya RCT. Researchers removed informational and registration barriers for 294 restaurants and 252 pharmacies by signing them up to Lipa na M-Pesa **for free and on their behalf**. Afterward **62% of restaurants and 21% of pharmacies** wanted to adopt. About 90% of the sample already knew Lipa na M-Pesa but had not adopted it. — [Tilburg/DFID PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5aaa3e6bed915d4f595c5a5a/Payment_technology_adoption_by_SME.pdf)
  - Stated reasons for not adopting: "not seeing the benefits," "too costly to open an account," "high transaction fees," "don't have time to open an account," "would not increase my sales," "no trust in provider," "too complex." Restaurants cited lack of time for paperwork but also doubted any sales benefit, which suggests that doing the paperwork for them was decisive. — same source
  - **Visibility/tax proxy:** firms that refused to share sales figures with enumerators were about **13 percentage points** less likely to adopt, and those refusing to share profits about **18 pp** less likely. The authors conclude that "lower visibility concerns" predict adoption. — same source
  - Effect: treatment raised cashless usage by about **40%** relative to control and raised monthly Lipa na M-Pesa sales by **26%** (about KSh 3,256 / US$32, roughly 15% of average SME sales). Sixteen months later, adopters "continue using it and feel safer." The fee at the time was 1% of the payment value. — same source
  - Context in the same paper: by 2014 only 35–40% of Kenyan SMEs used M-Pesa for P2B/B2B. Theft (employee and shoplifting) cost about 1.99% of sales. — same source
- CGAP blog (Pillai, Baatjies, Faz; 26 Sep 2025), "Why Merchants Still Choose Cash in Sub-Saharan Africa." It counts about 44M MSMEs in SSA; more than 95% are micro and more than 80% are informal, often with single-digit margins. MDR (merchant discount rate) runs 0.5–3%. On US$10 of goods at a ~10% margin, a 3% fee removes about **one third of profit**. Barriers it lists: fees, **tax scrutiny fear** (Kenya and Uganda already analyze P2P and merchant data), **liquidity** (cash is instant, while disputes and reversals take days; Nigeria has a 72-hour dispute window), **KYC** (registration, tax ID, address), **trust** (downtime; staff cannot see payment confirmations), and payments sold as an isolated service with no added value. Recommendations: free tiers for small merchants or charging the customer instead (Tanzania example), tiered KYC "informal trader wallets" (MTN, Pochi la Biashara), bundling with business tools, and tax incentives (Mexico, Colombia, India, Argentina). — [CGAP](https://www.cgap.org/blog/why-merchants-still-choose-cash-in-sub-saharan-africa)
- CGAP slide deck (2017): the most common merchant complaint is lack of access to capital. Tax reporting and informality are also listed as barriers. — [CGAP 2017](https://www.cgap.org/sites/default/files/publications/slidedeck/digitizingmerchantpaymentswhatwillittake-170907135915.pdf)
- World Bank Merchant Payments & DFS Handbook (2021): digital payments leave an electronic trace that makes revenue verifiable, and many small merchants avoid digital payments for this reason. — [World Bank](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/358361613558168171/pdf/Merchant-Payments-and-Digital-Financial-Services-Handbook.pdf). Counterpoint: TQH Consulting (India, 2017) found merchants "not overwhelmingly concerned" about tax scrutiny. — [TQH](https://thequantumhub.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/Addressing-the-barriers-to-adoption-in-digital-payments-TQH-Consulting-Aug2017-Final.pdf)
- Ghana (World Bank Enterprise Survey data): **26.89% of informal firms extend trade credit to customers**, and digital payment adoption raises the likelihood of extending credit. — [PMC article](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11168480/)

- From Aug 2026 ("PATA MORE"), Safaricom made Buy Goods payments up to KSh 500 fee-free for merchants and ran a time-limited free Pochi band up to KSh 200. — [Tech-ish](https://tech-ish.com/2026/08/01/mpesa-pochi-buy-goods-tariff-cuts-2026/); [TechTrendsKE](https://techtrendske.co.ke/2026/08/01/safaricom-pochi-la-biashara-ksh200-free-payments/)

- Vendor blogs (low reliability, conflicting numbers): Orange Money and MTN MoMo are said to dominate, with an Orange share variously given as 52% or ~35%. MTN reportedly launched MoMo Pay QR merchant acceptance in 2023. — [simiz.io](https://simiz.io/blog/marche-paiement-mobile-cameroun-2026)

**West Africa (Wave)**
- Wave's 1% P2P fee and free cash-in/cash-out forced Orange and MTN to cut to 1% in Senegal and Côte d'Ivoire. This shows how strongly price drives behavior in francophone Africa. — [Rest of World 2022](https://restofworld.org/2022/how-wave-is-disrupting-francophone-africas-mobile-money-market/); [GSMA 2024](https://www.gsma.com/solutions-and-impact/connectivity-for-good/mobile-for-development/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/Disruption-vs-sustainability_-Lessons-from-mobile-money-in-West-Africa.pdf). Vendor blog: Wave merchant fee about 1% (Senegal) to about 1.5% (Côte d'Ivoire), with D+1/D+2 payout. — [Kolonell](https://kolonell.com/en/blog/wave-ivory-coast-vs-senegal-merchant-fees-comparison-2026) (unverified)

### Inferences
- **Onboarding matters more than persuasion.** The Kenya RCT shows that most of the gap is friction (paperwork, time), not opposition. MoloPay should offer an assisted, agent-done onboarding: the field agent fills in the form, photographs the products, and sets up the catalogue in one visit.
- **Fee framing is critical.** Kenyan merchants moved en masse to a product where they keep 100% of the amount received. MoloPay's commission should be presented as payment for a sale MoloPay brought in (a customer who already saved toward the product), not as a deduction from the merchant's own sale. The comparison should be against the cost of informal credit losses and dead stock.
- **Tax visibility fear is real, and its strength varies.** The Kenyan non-disclosure proxy cut adoption by 13–18 pp. MoloPay should spell out clearly what data it shares and with whom, and avoid tax-authority-like language such as "déclaration."
- **The CEMAC opportunity is large but early.** 30.9M accounts exist in Cameroon, but merchant payments are dominated by airtime. Customer wallets exist; merchant habits do not yet.

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous Cameroon- or CEMAC-specific study of merchant (P2B) acceptance or merchant fees. GSMA State of the Industry merchant figures for Central Africa were not retrieved.
- Nigeria POS academic evidence and Kudi details were not found within the time budget. Twiga, TradeDepot, MarketForce and Lami were not researched (out of time). Their B2B lessons, including MarketForce's and Twiga's difficulties, should be checked separately.

## 2. Informal shopkeeper credit, unpaid debts, layaway / lay-by, savings-to-purchase and PAYG

### Takeaway
Shopkeeper credit is widespread: it was the largest credit source for Kenyan households in 2019, and 27% of informal Ghanaian firms extend it. It mostly hurts cash flow rather than causing large write-offs. South African lay-by is legally protected (CPA s.62) and is making a comeback, but traditional lay-by suffers from admin costs and non-completion. PAYG and asset finance players (Watu, M-KOPA) built large dealer and agent networks, with dealers gaining financed volume.

### Cited Findings
- Kenya: loans from shopkeepers were the **largest share of household credit in 2019**. Between May 2020 and Jul 2021, **33% of Kenyan households** bought food on credit from shopkeepers (FSD Kenya, as summarized in search results). — [FSD Kenya presentation 2020](https://www.fsdkenya.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/Synthesis-presentation_24.08.2020_final.pdf)
- FSD Kenya (Aug 2020) on women and youth grocery traders: **slow repayment by credit customers was a major challenge**, made worse when customers lost jobs. — [FSD Kenya](https://www.fsdkenya.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/Synthesis-presentation_24.08.2020_final.pdf)
- Tanzania trade credit providers: **68% reported defaults and 32% late payments**. Outright losses were often **below 5% of sales**, but the effects on cash flow were significant. — [ResearchGate, Tanzania](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/397645951_Trade_Credit_as_a_Catalyst_for_Small_and_Medium-Sized_Enterprise_Performance_and_Growth_Insights_from_Trade_Credit_Providers_in_Tanzania)
- Ghana: **26.89%** of informal firms extend trade credit to customers. — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11168480/)
- South Africa (Polokwane): debtor control systems at small retailers are often poorly implemented or manipulated, and so ineffective. — [Fatoki, J Soc Sci](http://krepublishers.com/02-Journals/JSS/JSS-45-0-000-15-Web/JSS-45-3-15-Abst-PDF/JSS-45-3-177-15-1974-Fatoki-O-O/JSS-45-3-177-15-1974-Fatoki-O-O-Tx[2].pdf)
- South Africa lay-by: no credit check and no interest; regulated by Consumer Protection Act section 62. — [The Citizen 2021](https://www.pressreader.com/south-africa/the-citizen-gauteng/20210127/281771336857435). Truworths started offering lay-byes in 2018. — [Business Day](https://www.pressreader.com/south-africa/business-day/20180817/281500752092847). Game's standard terms are 3 months with no fees (sponsored content). — [IOL](https://www.iol.co.za/news/partnered/6-tips-on-how-to-make-your-money-go-further-in-january-de32f1f7-38f6-476c-bf3b-517e9e3e45cf)
- Company claim (LayUp, a digital lay-by provider in SA): traditional lay-by has **high admin costs and high non-completion**, and LayUp says it can raise finalisation rates to **about 60%**. — [Business Report 2020](https://businessreport.co.za/personal-finance/financial-planning/2020-12-09-payment-alternatives-to-buying-on-credit); [IT-Online](https://it-online.co.za/?p=165617)
- Vendor commentary (Altron FinTech, 2026): "Lay-by is back" as retailers digitise it to drive sales. — [ITWeb](https://www.itweb.co.za/office/altronfintech/content/DZQ58vV8mroMzXy2)
- Consumer context: 55% of South Africans live paycheque to paycheque (McKinsey survey of 1,000). — [McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/ng/our-insights/south-africas-cautious-consumer)
- **Watu** (motorcycle asset finance, company/press): more than **400 authorised dealers in Kenya** (Aug 2024); an earlier figure was more than 300 (Jan 2024). It has financed **more than 500,000 motorcycles** in Kenya, against a national fleet of about 2.5M boda bodas. A claim states that **80–90% of motorcycles are bought on finance** in most of its markets. It partnered with Spiro (Jan 2024) to finance e-bikes through its dealer network. Profits dropped 85% (2025, TechCabal). — [The Star 2024](https://www.the-star.co.ke/news/2024-08-04-watu-to-finance-10000-electric-bikes-in-2024-report); [HapaKenya](https://hapakenya.com/2024/01/24/watu-credit-partners-with-spiro-in-a-bid-to-enhance-financing-for-electric-bikes); [TechCabal](https://techcabal.com/2025/05/22/watu-profits-drop-85-1-2-million/)
- **M-KOPA** (company/press): reached **10M customers** (Jul 2026). It reports between 30,000 and about 50,000 commission-paid sales agents (sources conflict). Deposit is typically **10–15% of the financed handset cost** (GSMA, 2021 deal). — [TechCabal 2026](https://techcabal.com/2026/07/22/m-kopa-reaches-10-million-customers/); [GSMA 2022](https://www.gsma.com/mobilefordevelopment/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/M-KOPA-Applying-the-pay-as-you-go-model-to-smartphones-in-Africa.pdf); [TechCrunch 2024](https://techcrunch.com/2024/11/28/financial-inclusion-drives-m-kopa-to-400m-in-arr)

### Inferences
- **Lead with the cash-flow pain of informal credit.** The evidence is about delayed money and stress more than large write-offs. "With MoloPay, the customer pays before taking the product, and you never chase a debt again" uses loss aversion credibly. Overclaiming large losses would not be credible; Tanzania losses were under 5% of sales.
- **Expect non-completion and plan for it.** Lay-by's weakness is that customers abandon their plans. The merchant portal should show the pipeline honestly (savers on track, at risk, abandoned), and the policy for abandoned savings must be clear to merchants: is stock reserved or not? Frozen price risk also needs a clear rule, such as a maximum duration or an inflation clause.
- **Model the dealer channel on Watu and M-KOPA.** Dealers adopt when a third party brings them financed or committed buyers and pays them in full at handover. MoloPay's "paid at handover" promise matches this pattern.

### Gaps
- I found no quantified survey of SA lay-by usage share or of merchant gains, and no Cameroon data on shop credit ("crédit"/"ardoise") prevalence or losses.
- No independent data on what dealers gained from Watu or M-KOPA partnerships (sales uplift, payout speed).

## 3. BNPL in Africa: partner value propositions and adoption

### Takeaway
Independent merchant-adoption data is scarce. Company figures are modest: Payflex reported about 1,000–1,500 merchants. In Cameroon the only visible precedent is Access Bank's "Pick Now Pay Later," which uses Glotelho as its single approved merchant. Glotelho's own instalment scheme has drawn complaints about hidden fees and phone locking, which is a reputational warning for MoloPay.

### Cited Findings
- Payflex (SA, founded 2017; acquired by Zip in 2021): **more than 1,000 merchants and 135,000 customers** (2021), with a later claim of more than 1,500 online merchants and 200,000+ users. These are company claims. — [TechCrunch 2021](https://techcrunch.com/2021/09/03/zip-acquisition-of-payflex-means-africa-is-ripe-for-bnpl-disruption/); [Silicon Africa](https://siliconafrica.org/bnpl-buy-now-pay-later-startups-in-africa/)
- Lipa Later (Kenya): licensed digital credit provider under the Central Bank of Kenya since March 2024. Merchant count not found. — [TechBuild Africa](https://techbuild.africa/buy-now-pay-later-in-africa/)
- Easybuy and CredPal: integrated as BNPL at Jumia Nigeria checkout (May 2024). CDcare offers zero-interest instalments on appliances and vehicles. No merchant counts were found. — [TechBuild Africa](https://techbuild.africa/buy-now-pay-later-in-africa/); [The Condia](https://thecondia.com/gadget-purchases-buy-now-pay-later/)
- Market report (ResearchAndMarkets 2026, low reliability): forecasts African BNPL at US$16.8bn by 2029 and names Payflex, Lipa Later, valU and CredPal as anchors. — [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/africa-buy-now-pay-later-154400500.html)
- **Cameroon:** Access Bank Cameroon "Pick Now Pay Later" offers salary-backed instalments on electronics and furniture, with a **3-month interest-free moratorium** and **48-hour approval**. Purchases are currently possible only at the approved supplier, **Glotelho**. — [Access Bank Cameroon](https://cameroon.accessbankplc.com/personal/loans/pick-now-pay-later)
- Glotelho instalment complaints: customers report **hidden fees revealed after signing** and **phone blocking** after instalment payments. — [237online](https://www.237online.com/glotelho-paiement-echelonne-manquements/)

### Inferences
- MoloPay's structure (customer saves first, merchant paid at handover, BNPL only through a licensed lender) avoids credit risk for the merchant. That is a clear point against "I will be stuck with unpaid instalments." The Cameroonian Glotelho precedent shows consumer anger at hidden fees spills onto the merchant brand, so full fee transparency protects partners too.

### Gaps
- No partner-side metrics (conversion uplift, average order value uplift, merchant discount rates) from any African BNPL provider. Chpter, PalmCredit and Paga BNPL were not researched.

## 4. Behavioral levers for B2B merchant adoption

### Takeaway
The strongest documented levers are: removing effort (done-for-you onboarding), zero or near-zero merchant-side fees or charging the customer, fast and visible payout (T+1 or better), peer network effects, and bundling payments with tools merchants value (credit, records). Merchants who adopt report feeling "safer."

### Cited Findings
- **Effort and simplicity:** free sign-up done on the merchant's behalf produced 62% / 21% willingness to adopt (Kenya RCT). — [Dalton et al. 2018](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5aaa3e6bed915d4f595c5a5a/Payment_technology_adoption_by_SME.pdf)
- **Social proof / network effects:** merchant acceptance and consumer adoption reinforce each other (+21% consumer card adoption; sales shift toward small shops). — [Higgins 2024](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20201952). Prior mobile money exposure predicts adoption. — [Dalton et al.](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5aaa3e6bed915d4f595c5a5a/Payment_technology_adoption_by_SME.pdf)
- **Fee incidence:** merchants prefer products where they "receive the full amount" (Pochi growth of 72.6% vs 32.2% for Buy Goods). — [Nation](https://beta.nation.co.ke/kenya/business/-pochi-la-biashara-outpaces-buy-goods-as-traders-shun-fees-5257948). CGAP recommends free tiers or charging the customer. — [CGAP 2025](https://www.cgap.org/blog/why-merchants-still-choose-cash-in-sub-saharan-africa)
- **Safety and security:** RCT adopters "feel safer" after 16 months. — [Dalton et al.](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5aaa3e6bed915d4f595c5a5a/Payment_technology_adoption_by_SME.pdf). Moniepoint's company survey reports 96% improved transaction security. — [Techeconomy](https://techeconomy.ng/beyond-payments-how-moniepoint-powered-nigerias-msme-growth-in-2025)
- **Liquidity and payout speed:** cash is instant, and disputes and reversals harm merchants; Nigeria has a 72-hour dispute window. — [CGAP 2025](https://www.cgap.org/blog/why-merchants-still-choose-cash-in-sub-saharan-africa)
- **Bundled value:** access to capital is the most common merchant complaint. — [CGAP 2017](https://www.cgap.org/sites/default/files/publications/slidedeck/digitizingmerchantpaymentswhatwillittake-170907135915.pdf). Moniepoint used payment data to give 3 in 4 borrowers their first formal credit (company claim). — [Guardian NG](https://guardian.ng/?p=2884068). Bundling with payroll, supplier payments and cash-flow tools is recommended. — [CGAP 2025](https://www.cgap.org/blog/why-merchants-still-choose-cash-in-sub-saharan-africa)
- **Staff access:** owners only can see confirmations, which creates trust issues with employees, so CGAP recommends employee-accessible confirmations. — [CGAP 2025](https://www.cgap.org/blog/why-merchants-still-choose-cash-in-sub-saharan-africa)

### Inferences (portal and pitch design)
- **Dashboard:** show "money already saved by your customers for your products" (committed future revenue) and the expected handover dates. This makes MoloPay feel like guaranteed future sales, which addresses the "not seeing the benefit" barrier.
- **Fee display:** show the commission per sale as a single figure in FCFA, next to what the merchant receives. Avoid percentages alone.
- **Payout speed:** promise and display payout at handover (instant mobile money). Matching Wave's and Moniepoint's T+0/T+1 norms is the minimum.
- **Staff access:** add a cashier or employee role that can verify a handover code without seeing the balances. This addresses CGAP's trust point.
- **Peer proof:** list neighbouring partners by quartier (district) or market; network effects are local.
- **Avoid tax-like language** and state the data-sharing policy plainly.

### Gaps
- No independent experiment found on dashboards, forecasting or social comparison specifically for African merchants. Twiga, TradeDepot, MarketForce, Kudi and Lami evidence not retrieved.

## 5. Landlords and schools: third-party payment platforms

### Takeaway
Schools adopt collection platforms readily when the platform is free to the school and gives real-time reconciliation. Uganda's SchoolPay reports about 14,000 schools. Nigerian landlords strongly prefer lump-sum annual rent upfront, so rent-financing models that pay landlords upfront struggled with sustainability. That matches MoloPay's "paid in full at handover" logic.

### Cited Findings
- **Uganda SchoolPay** (company/press): the school management system is provided **free to schools**, with **real-time visibility of all payments from any channel** (MTN, Airtel, banks). Reported reach is **more than 14,000 schools**. It is run by Fincom, a payment operator licensed by the Bank of Uganda. Schools cite digitised records as a benefit. — [Daily Monitor](https://www.monitor.co.ug/uganda/business/technology/school-fees-digital-payments-and-the-story-of-long-queues-4381654); [UNCDF](https://www.uncdf.org/article/8166/extending-the-frontiers-of-digital-school-fees-payments)
- SchoolPay added a school-fees **savings** product with Airtel Money and Sanlam (Aug 2025). - **Nigeria rent:** tenants must pay 1–2 years upfront, plus agency and legal fees of up to 25%. Landlords prefer annual lump sums because they **cut admin costs and default risk** and can be invested to beat inflation. Spleet's loans carry about **3.5% monthly interest**, and it reportedly could serve only about **10% of applications**. ### Inferences
- **For landlords:** pitch MoloPay as "the tenant saves the deposit / annual rent with us, and you receive it in full on move-in." This keeps the lump-sum preference intact and removes vetting effort. Do not ask landlords to accept monthly payment.
- **For schools:** a free bursar view with real-time reconciliation of which parents have saved how much toward next term's fees mirrors SchoolPay's winning formula. A commission deducted from fees may face resistance, so consider who pays it (parent or school).
- **Motorcycle dealers:** see section 2 (Watu). Dealers value volume plus full payment at sale.
